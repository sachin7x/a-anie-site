Story

Why I stopped typing
and started building.

Sachin Dohdiya — Kishangarh, Ajmer, Rajasthan — 2026

The gap between thought and text.

I think faster than I type. That's not a complaint — it's a physical reality. Speaking happens at around 130 to 160 words per minute for most people who speak fluidly. Typing, even with years of practice, is usually 45 to 80. The delta is not a productivity gap — it's a cognitive tax every time you open a document.

I knew the numbers. I had tried every voice-to-text tool I could find. And every single one of them had the same problem: they transcribed what I said. Not what I meant.

"uhhh so like the meeting is — no wait, can we move it to Wednesday?"
Every tool I tried: "uhhh so like the meeting is — no wait, can we move it to Wednesday?"
What I meant: "Let's move the meeting to Wednesday."

I was spending as much time cleaning up dictation as I would have spent just typing. The tools were transcribing faithfully, not usefully.

The accent problem.

The second problem was harder to articulate until I felt it: every tool I tried was built for a particular kind of English. American English, read clearly, with short sentences. My English is different. Not wrong — just different.

My name, "Dohdiya", would come out as "Daudia" or "Doh-dee-ah" or "Dohjiya" — never right. The name of my city, "Kishangarh", would break into two or three words and lose all meaning. And Hinglish — the code-switching between Hindi and English that half of India does naturally — was simply not supported.

A tool that doesn't understand how you speak isn't a dictation tool. It's a transcription machine that needs an editor.

I needed an editor, not a transcriber.

The obvious answer, to me, was to build it. Not to wait for the market to catch up, not to pick from the available options, but to build the version I actually needed.

What A-Anie actually does.

The core difference is in what happens between speech and text. A traditional speech-to-text engine has one job: turn audio into words. A-Anie has two: understand the intent, then produce the text that intent deserves.

What the engine hears

"bhai kal ki meeting postpone kar do, client busy hai — oh wait, actually day after tomorrow bhi possible hai, check karo"

↓ A-Anie processes ↓
Output

"Please check if the day after tomorrow works — the client mentioned being available then."

That's filler removal, self-correction resolution, language detection, and translation — all happening in one pass, before the text lands in your document. The output doesn't need a human editor because the editing was done by the tool.

That's what "writes what you meant" means, concretely.

Built solo. On purpose.

I chose to build this alone. Not because I couldn't find collaborators, but because the product needed a single, coherent point of view on what "good" output looks like. Voice-to-text is a personal preference problem as much as a technical one. The right edit for a Hinglish email is not the right edit for a formal English report. Having one person make those decisions keeps the product honest.

It also means the user emails me. Not a support queue. Not a ticket tracker. The person who wrote the code, who set the tone rules, who chose the Hindi-English translation defaults — that's who you reach when you have a question.

The tradeoff is real: there is no 24-hour SLA, there is no dedicated support team, and there are bugs that take longer to fix because one person has only so many hours in a day. The tradeoff is worth it, to me, because the alternative was a product that tried to please everyone and pleased no one well.

The thesis: English is the new programming language.

The clearest articulation of why a product like A-Anie is necessary comes from Jeroen Rosenberg's essay, "English is the new programming language". The argument: with AI in the loop, the bottleneck is no longer syntax, library knowledge, or framework familiarity — it is the precision of the English description of intent. The "program" is increasingly the prompt; the source code is a generated artifact.

That thesis is exactly the problem A-Anie was built to solve. If the new bottleneck is "stating your intent clearly," then the value of a tool that helps you state it well — in Hinglish, with self-corrections, with the way you actually speak — is enormous. A-Anie is the layer between how a person thinks and what an AI can act on.
